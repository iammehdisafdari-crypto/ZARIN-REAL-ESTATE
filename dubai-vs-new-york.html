Head-to-Head

Why This Comparison Matters

New York and Dubai are the two skyscraper capitals of the global investor's imagination — the established giant and the twenty-five-year-old challenger. American buyers now rank among the top five foreign investor groups in Dubai by transaction volume, and the corridor runs both ways: Gulf sovereign wealth owns marquee Manhattan towers, while US private capital increasingly treats Dubai as the natural third leg of a global portfolio after New York and London. The two markets are closer than they first look: both price in dollars — the dirham has been pegged to the USD at 3.6725 since 1997, so Americans carry no currency risk in Dubai — both are English-language, common-law-adjacent environments, and both attract the same discerning buyers seeking yield plus lifestyle. The honest comparison is between the deepest, most liquid market on earth and the fastest-growing large market on earth.

On price, yield, and tax, Dubai wins decisively. Manhattan prime trades at USD 1,500-2,500 per square foot (AED 5,500-9,200) — three to four times Dubai's prime AED 2,200-2,800 in Downtown and Palm Jumeirah — and New York citywide averages ~USD 800/sqft (AED ~2,930) against Dubai's ~USD 408 (AED 1,500). Gross yields run 3.5-4.5% in New York versus 6-7% in Dubai (7.5% on Business Bay studios). The tax gap compounds annually: New York levies a Mansion Tax of 1-3.9% on purchases above USD 1M, combined transfer taxes of 1-2.6%, an annual property tax of roughly 1% of assessed value, capital gains of 15-20% federal plus 8.82% New York State on exit, and a 40% federal estate tax above the exemption. Dubai charges 4% at purchase, nothing annually, nothing on gains, nothing at death. New York answers with unmatched liquidity — a majority-renter city of 8.5M with the deepest resale market in North America — and two centuries of institutional depth.

Dubai suits yield-driven investors, tax-sensitive owners, and Americans seeking a warm-weather second base with zero currency risk: the USD peg means a Dubai apartment behaves like a dollar asset, which is precisely why US nationals now sit among Dubai's top five buyer nationalities. The Golden Visa (AED 2M, 10-year renewable) adds a residency dimension New York cannot offer — the US has no property-led immigration route, and EB-5 requires a USD 800K+ job-creating business investment. New York suits buyers who need liquidity above all — the ability to sell a USD 10M apartment in ninety days — plus those whose careers, families and time zones are anchored in the Americas. The pairing is common: a Manhattan condo as the core holding, a Dubai unit at 6-7% yield as the cash-flow sleeve. ZARIN's founder has been investing in Dubai real estate since 2005, and the numbers below show why that conviction held through every cycle.

Reading the Segment Table

At every tier, Dubai delivers roughly three times the space or specification for the same money. A USD 545K budget (AED 2M) buys a Manhattan studio or a well-located Brooklyn one-bedroom — in Dubai, a full 1BR in Business Bay or a JVC two-bedroom with pool, gym and covered parking. At USD 2M, New York offers a family 2-3BR in Brooklyn or Long Island City; Dubai offers a Dubai Hills 3-4BR townhouse, or a high-floor Marina 2BR plus a second entry-level investment unit. The prime tier is where the markets rhyme most: Billionaires' Row and Palm Jumeirah are both global trophy corridors, but the Dubai unit prices at AED 2,800/sqft against Manhattan's AED 5,500-9,200. Note the ownership distinction: most Manhattan stock below the luxury tier is co-op — shares in a corporation, with board interviews, sublet restrictions and resale approvals — while Dubai apartments are freehold title with no board at all. For a hands-off investor, that difference alone can decide the comparison.

Why Dubai's Net Advantage Is Larger Than Gross Suggests

New York's carrying costs are the yield killer. A USD 545K Brooklyn one-bedroom grosses 4.5% (USD 24,500), but common charges of USD 900-1,200 a month, a ~0.9%-effective property tax, insurance and management take roughly USD 18,700 off the top, and depreciation-adjusted income tax trims the rest — the net settles near 1% (0.9-1.3% depending on tax position), before any mortgage. Dubai's AED 2M equivalent nets about 5.2% — four to five times the cash flow. The deeper issue is tax drag over the hold: New York's annual property tax (~1%) plus capital gains at exit (15-20% federal plus 8.82% state, plus FIRPTA mechanics for non-resident sellers) compounds against you, while Dubai's 0%-0%-0% regime lets the full rent and the full gain accrue. The one structural equaliser: the AED-USD peg means an American investor in Dubai carries no currency risk in either direction — a rarity among international yield markets, and a quiet, decisive advantage for dollar-earning buyers.

Tax & Ownership

Tax & Ownership Deep Dive

The four tax heads that decide cross-border property returns: what you pay to get in, what you pay to hold, what you pay to get out, and what your heirs pay when you are gone. Compared side by side, city against city, with the 2024-2026 rule changes baked in.

Tax Head 01

Purchase Taxes & Fees

Dubai: a 4% DLD fee, ~2% commission and AED 4,580 admin — roughly 6% flat. New York stacks a Mansion Tax of 1-3.9% on all purchases above USD 1M, combined state and city transfer taxes of 1-2.6%, attorney fees of USD 3,000-5,000, and title insurance (~0.5%); co-op purchases add board-package legal costs, and some buildings levy flip taxes on exit. All-in, a USD 2M Manhattan condo bears roughly USD 80-100K in taxes and fees — comparable in percentage, on a far larger ticket.

Tax Head 02

Ongoing Taxes

Dubai: no annual property tax — building service charges only. New York levies property tax every year at roughly 1% of assessed value (effective rates vary 0.4-1.2% by property class), plus condo common charges or co-op maintenance — much of it non-deductible. A USD 1.5M condo commonly carries USD 12,000-18,000 in combined annual carrying costs before any mortgage. Over a ten-year hold, New York's recurring levies alone can exceed Dubai's entire one-time 4% transfer fee.

Tax Head 03

Exit Taxes (Capital Gains)

Dubai: 0% capital gains tax — the full gain accrues to the seller. A US seller of New York property owes federal long-term capital gains of 15-20% (plus the 3.8% net investment income tax above thresholds) and New York State tax up to 8.82% — a combined marginal rate near 32.6% for top earners. Foreign sellers additionally face FIRPTA withholding of 15% of the gross sale price at closing, reclaimed only through a US tax filing.

Tax Head 04

Succession & Inheritance

Dubai charges 0% inheritance tax; a registered DIFC Will passes UAE assets under English-law principles. The US levies a 40% estate tax above the exemption (USD 13.6M per person in 2024, indexed) — and here is the trap for foreign owners: non-citizen, non-resident owners of US-situs assets such as New York condos receive only a USD 60,000 exemption. A USD 5M Manhattan condo held directly by a Dubai resident can face roughly USD 1.97M of US estate tax on death. Structuring matters more in New York than in almost any other market.

Tax rules change. This comparison reflects published rules as of early 2026; ZARIN's cross-border tax desk verifies each client's position with qualified advisors in both jurisdictions before any purchase — the consultation is free and without obligation.

Lifestyle & Practical

Lifestyle & Practical Considerations

Property is a life decision before it is a spreadsheet decision. Four honest comparisons — climate, schooling, healthcare, and safety and language — where both cities have genuine strengths and neither is objectively "better".

Consideration 01

Climate

New York delivers four true seasons — snowy ~0°C winters, hot 28-32°C humid summers, and genuinely beautiful spring and autumn. Dubai splits the year in two: a perfect October-to-April (24-32°C, daily sun) and a fierce June-to-September (38-42°C). Americans used to seasons often buy Dubai on snowbird logic — for the winter half-year — while keeping New York for summer. The honest summary: Dubai wins November to March, New York wins May to September, and the rest is preference.

Consideration 02

Schooling

New York runs America's most celebrated public schools (Stuyvesant, Bronx Science) alongside elite privates (Dalton, Trinity at USD 50,000-60,000+) and the world's deepest university pipeline. Dubai's 200+ private schools — British, IB and American curricula at AED 40,000-100,000 — offer smaller classes, modern campuses and far easier admissions, with NYU and other US institutions now present in the UAE. For K-12 ceilings, New York is higher; for a stress-free, English-language, college-prep education, Dubai is the easier city.

Consideration 03

Healthcare

New York is one of medicine's global capitals — Memorial Sloan Kettering, Mount Sinai, NewYork-Presbyterian — with superb outcomes for the well-insured; self-employed and international buyers should budget USD 6,000-15,000+ a year for family coverage. Dubai's insurance-based private system — the American Hospital, Mediclinic, Rashid — delivers fast, high-standard, English-language care with mandatory cover from ~AED 5,000-10,000, and has become a medical-tourism destination itself. Both cities will look after you excellently; New York is the frontier, Dubai the efficient market.

Consideration 04

Safety & Language

New York is the safest big city in America, with crime near sixty-year lows — though normal urban precautions apply, particularly at night. Dubai ranks among the top ten safest cities globally (safety index in the high 80s), where violent crime is statistically negligible. Language is seamless in both: English dominates Dubai's contracts, schooling and business exactly as it does New York's. For an American, neither city presents any language, contract or daily-living friction worth worrying about — the decision comes down to economics and lifestyle.

The Decision

Which City Is Right for You?

Five reasons each way, with no sales agenda — roughly a third of ZARIN's advisory conversations end with "buy in your home market" or "buy both", and that is the correct answer for many buyers.

Choose Dubai if…

  • Yield is the priority — 6-7% gross (7.5% Business Bay studios) against New York's 3.5-4.5% — roughly 5.2% versus ~1% net after carrying costs.
  • Tax efficiency matters — 0% capital gains, 0% annual property tax and 0% inheritance tax, against 15-20% federal gains plus 8.82% state, ~1% annual property tax and a 40% estate tax.
  • You want zero currency risk — the dirham is pegged to the dollar at 3.6725, so a Dubai asset is functionally a USD asset for an American.
  • You want residency — the AED 2M Golden Visa runs 10 years, renewable; the US offers no property-led green card — EB-5 needs a USD 800K+ business investment.
  • You prefer frictionless ownership — freehold title with no co-op board interviews, no sublet restrictions, and new-build services as standard.

Choose New York if…

  • Liquidity is the priority — the deepest, most liquid residential market on earth, with institutional-scale exits possible in weeks at almost any price point.
  • You value institutional depth — two centuries of title records, the world's largest REIT and appraisal ecosystem, and the most sophisticated financing market anywhere.
  • Your life is anchored in the Americas — New York's time zone bridges Europe and Latin America, and the city remains the default base for US-centric careers.
  • You want rental depth — a majority-renter city of 8.5M people provides occupancy resilience no Gulf market can yet match.
  • Cultural capital matters — theatres, museums, restaurants and professional networks at a scale only London and Tokyo approach.

Is Dubai really cheaper than New York?
Yes — three to four times cheaper at the prime tier. Manhattan prime (Billionaires' Row, Tribeca, Soho) trades at USD 1,500-2,500 per square foot (AED 5,500-9,200) against AED 2,200-2,800 for prime Downtown Dubai and Palm Jumeirah. Citywide, New York averages ~USD 800/sqft (AED ~2,930) against Dubai's ~USD 408 (AED 1,500). In budget terms: USD 1M buys a compact Manhattan one-bedroom or co-op studio — or a 2-3BR with pool, gym and parking in Dubai Hills Estate or Business Bay. New York's honest counterpoints are depth (every price band imaginable across 8.5M people) and the co-op discount — older co-ops can trade 30-40% below comparable condos — but a co-op is shares in a corporation with board approval, not freehold title. On space and specification per dollar, Dubai wins decisively.
Can I buy property in both Dubai and New York?
Yes — neither city restricts foreign ownership of the standard investor product. Dubai freehold zones are open to all nationalities; New York condos are freely purchasable by foreigners, while co-ops often restrict buyers through board interviews, sublet rules and resale approvals — which is exactly why international buyers concentrate in condos. The purchases are independent: separate title systems, financing and tax exposure. Americans buying Dubai should note FATCA reporting — UAE banks ask for W-9s and some decline US persons, though ZARIN maintains a US-person-friendly lender list — and US persons remain taxable by the IRS on worldwide income and gains, including Dubai rents. Foreign buyers of New York face FIRPTA withholding on sale and the USD 60,000 estate-tax exemption on US-situs assets. Both structures are manageable with the right advisors.
What tax does a US citizen or resident pay on a Dubai property?
US persons are taxed on worldwide income — the UAE's 0% regime does not exempt Americans. A US owner of a Dubai rental reports the rent on Schedule E (or through an owned entity), benefiting from depreciation, mortgage-interest and expense deductions that typically shelter much of a 6-7% gross yield; on sale, US capital gains of 15-20% apply even though the UAE charges nothing. The good news is structural: the USD-AED peg removes currency gain/loss noise, and the absence of UAE property tax keeps the filing simple. The critical step is professional structuring before purchase — a pass-through entity chosen with a cross-border CPA can legally minimise the double-reporting burden. ZARIN's cross-border tax desk works with US-qualified CPAs on exactly this.
Which city has better rental yields — Dubai or New York?
Dubai, by roughly 1.5-2× gross and 4-5× net. Dubai averages 6-7% gross citywide (7.5% on Business Bay studios) against New York's 3.5-4.5%. The net gap is where it compounds: a New York condo's common charges (USD 800-1,200 a month on a typical one-bed), ~0.9-1%-effective annual property tax, insurance and management consume roughly three-quarters of gross rent, netting near 1% before income tax and any mortgage; Dubai's service charges and management net about 5.2% with no property or income tax in the UAE. New York's defences are rental resilience — a majority-renter city of 8.5M with unmatched demand depth — and its resale liquidity. For pure cash flow, Dubai wins; for liquidity, New York.
Does buying property give me residency in Dubai or the US?
Dubai: yes — AED 2M of property secures the 10-year renewable Golden Visa, with 2-year investor visas from AED 750K and retirement routes from age 55. The US: no — there is no property-led green card; the EB-5 programme requires a minimum USD 800K (targeted employment area) to USD 1.05M investment that creates at least ten jobs, and passive real estate ownership has never qualified. This is one of the sharpest asymmetries in global property: an American can buy ten years of UAE residency for ~USD 545K, while a Dubai resident cannot buy US residency with any property sum. For buyers weighing a future base, schooling access or a plan-B jurisdiction, Dubai's Golden Visa is often the deciding factor.
Can I get a mortgage in Dubai as a non-resident — and in New York as a Dubai-based buyer?
Both, with different mechanics. Dubai non-resident mortgages typically run 50-60% LTV over up to 25 years at roughly 4-5% (2026 rates), with income verified from any country; USD-denominated Dubai mortgages exist alongside AED — practically identical thanks to the 3.6725 peg. New York finances non-resident foreigners at 50-65% LTV through bank foreign-national programmes, at rates modestly above domestic pricing, with no US credit history strictly required; co-ops often refuse financing entirely, another reason international buyers choose condos. The coverage equation is decisive: Dubai's 6-7% gross comfortably services a 4-5% coupon, while New York's 3.5-4.5% gross rarely covers carrying costs plus debt service. Leveraged buyers get further in Dubai.
What is the currency risk between AED and USD?
Effectively zero — and that is Dubai's quiet trump card for American buyers. The dirham has been pegged to the US dollar at 3.6725 since 1997, backed by the UAE's reserves, so a Dubai apartment is a dollar-denominated asset in every practical sense: no translation risk on purchase, rent, resale or repatriation. Compare the alternatives an American yield-buyer faces — sterling, euro, or emerging-market currency exposure — and Dubai is the only major international market offering dollar-grade currency stability with 6-7% yields. The peg's own risk is theoretical: a re-peg or float would require a macro shock the UAE's diversification is designed to withstand, and in twenty-five-plus years the rate has never moved. For dollar-based buyers, currency simply exits the conversation.
